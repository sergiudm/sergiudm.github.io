Software in the Next 3 Years, Part 1: The Bottleneck Is Now the Interface

Models and harnesses keep improving. The links around the agent have not: you still talk to it in prose, and it still reads your apps through screenshots. That is the new bottleneck.

Qijun Han November 11, 202612 min read

Agents can do about ten times more than they could two years ago.By METR’s measure, the length of task an agent can finish has doubled roughly every seven months since 2019. Two years of that is about 10x. Are you ten times more productive? Probably not. And I don’t think the model or the harness is what’s holding you back anymore.

The gap is not only a feeling. In a 2025 randomized study by METR, experienced open-source developers took 19% longer with AI tools, while believing they had been 20% faster. The models have improved a lot since then, and I am not saying agents don’t help. I am saying that what agents can do and what we get out of them are no longer growing at the same rate.

Nearly every improvement since ChatGPT came out has landed in two places: better models, and better harnesses around them (function calling, MCP, skills, subagents, memory, runs that go on for hours). This post is about the parts that did not improve, and why I think they are now the bottleneck.

The bottleneck moved#

In 2023, when an agent failed at a task, it was nearly always the agent’s own fault. It called the wrong tool, looped on the same action, or simply lost the instructions. We complained about short context windows, poor instruction following, and clumsy harnesses. The bottleneck was the agent itself.

Thanks to the whole AI community, agents improved on every one of those fronts, and we got bolder about what we hand them: first code completion, then whole pull requests, then essays and slide decks. Lately, always-on assistants such as Muse, Grok Bot, dots, and Duet have started to work proactively, around the clock. Their developers want to automate as much as possible, so that all you have to do is write a prompt and read an answer.

Now look at what stayed the same in the picture. We still write prompts and read answers. The agent still works on software from the outside. The way we talk to agents is almost the same as in 2023, and so is the way agents reach our software. The bottleneck has moved from the middle to the two links. If you have ever rewritten a prompt three times, or skimmed a 900-word answer for the one line you needed, you have felt it. That is what puts most of us on the flat line in the first picture.

There is one exception, and it proves the rule: coding. Coding is the one domain that partly escaped the chat box. In Cursor you press Tab to accept the next edit, so your intent travels in one key, and the agent reads where you are from your cursor. When an agent changes code, you check its work in one glance at a git diff, and the tests check it for you. Both links are wide. Canvas and Artifacts brought a little of this to writing and small apps. I don’t think it is a coincidence that coding agents feel magical, while agents that make slides, plan your week or drive desktop apps still feel clunky.

Let’s take the two links one at a time.

Bottleneck 1: prose is bad at structure#

Is a chat session good enough for talking to an agent? I don’t think so.

Two kinds of information pass between you and an agent:

  1. Content: what you actually care about. Which runs failed. Which task comes first.
  2. Structure: how that content is organized. A list of runs, each with a status and a loss. A task with a priority and a due date.

Natural language is good at content and bad at structure. When you write a long prompt, much of the effort goes into spelling out structure, and structure barely changes from one prompt to the next. That is exactly why it can be turned into patterns.

The other direction is no better. An agent’s answer is often long, and reading a long answer is slow and error-prone: you miss something, or you misread it. Try it:

The left side is not hard to read because it is badly written. It is hard because most of your effort goes into matching a structure you already know (run, learning rate, status, loss) before you reach the values you came for. The right side hands that job to your eyes. Every math teacher knows this: a Venn diagram beats a paragraph of set notation. For structured information, our brains prefer pictures to prose.

Machines have the same preference, for a different format:

Code and JSON are how machines read structure. A GUI is how human eyes read structure. Today we force both sides into their worst format: people read and write prose, and agents squint at screenshots.

My bet is that over the next few years, human efficiency on the user’s side will matter as much as token efficiency on the agent’s side, and that GUIs will become a new language for talking to agents. The more structured the information, the more a GUI will win over words. As a bonus, people who are not native speakers of the agent’s best language will get a much better experience.

Here is a view I find useful: a GUI is a representation of structured information, and in an agent’s world, that information is the app’s state. When you click a button or drag a card, you are not describing anything. You are changing the state directly. Which brings us to the second link.

Bottleneck 2: agents are locked outside the GUI#

We already have plenty of GUI software, so why not just use it? Because in today’s apps, only you can “speak” through the GUI. You click, and the state changes. The agent has no good way to speak back to you through the same surface, so it falls back on chat.

To see why, look at how agents use GUI software today. A computer-use agent (CUA) does what you ask by operating the computer the way you would: it looks at the screen, moves the mouse, and types. Since OSWorld gave the field a shared benchmark of real desktop tasks, CUAs have become much stronger, and they can now finish a wide range of them. But the way they work with a GUI doesn’t make much sense to me, for two reasons.

  • Pixel loops carry little information per step. For most closed-source apps, the agent learns the app’s state from screenshots. Even closing a popup means: take a high-resolution screenshot, have a model read it, click, and take another screenshot to check. Where an accessibility tree or a DOM is available, it describes how the screen is drawn, not the app’s state, it is noisy, and many desktop apps expose too little of it, so the agent falls back to screenshots anyway. Unlike the output of a shell command, each screenshot says very little, so a task takes many of them. Closing a popup and adding one task to a to-do app takes at least four screenshots (about 5,000 image tokens) and four sequential calls to a vision model, each of which can misread the screen or click a few pixels off.Anthropic’s vision docs estimate an image at about width × height / 750 tokens, so a 1280×720 screenshot is about 1,200 tokens. Models that read full-resolution screenshots can spend up to about 4,800 on one. In my own task app, the same change is one command of about twenty tokens, rmd add "Reply to reviewer 2" 8. And if the agent needs to know everything first, one call, rmd state, returns all that the app’s pages can show: about 34 KB of JSON, or roughly 10,000 tokens. That is the price of about eight screenshots, each of which would show one screen of one page.Measured on my own data with rmd state | wc -c. The token count is an estimate at 3 to 4 bytes per token.
  • GUIs have no git diff and no unit tests. When an agent writes code, we check its work with tests, version control, and a diff we can read at a glance. When an agent works through a GUI, there is nothing like that. Part of the reason is that GUI tasks take many small steps, and asking you to approve each one would bury you. So you either trust thirty clicks you didn’t watch, or you watch all thirty.

So, can we make GUI software agent-friendly? That means the agent needs only a few steps to act, and you can accept or reject the result at a glance. I think we can. The key is a bridge that lets the agent speak to the software directly, the way you do when you click. Then the UI responds to both of you.

Before you can work this way, the software has to be set up, and setting it up still takes natural language. But it is a lot like writing reusable skills for an agent: you describe a pattern once, and after that a key stands for it. The more of these we build, the fewer words we spend on the parts that never change.

Where this leaves us#

With agents improving this fast, the bottleneck is now how we communicate with them, at both ends. People love to click and press Tab, yet we write long prompts and read long answers. Agents love to write code and call tools, yet they take screenshots and go the visual way. Each side is stuck working in the other’s format.

In Part 2, I will go through practical ways to cut the friction at both links: what existing work such as MCP, generative UI and CLI wrappers already fixes and what it leaves open, some new ideas, and the proof of concept I built around them. That is LaunchX, a launcher whose apps hand their state straight to your agents, together with Schedule Everything, the app from the demo above.
